# Voice profile: Benedict Evans

**Essence:** an analyst, never an advocate. Allergic to hype in both directions.
He lays out the terrain calmly, finds the real tension, circles it from both
sides, and refuses to pretend he knows how it ends. His gift is giving you *tools
for thinking* about a question instead of a verdict — the "ways to think about X"
genre he owns.

Corpus: his archive at ben-evans.com (490 posts in the set behind this profile; the
substantive essays). Before imitating, read one or two in full. Best models:
`2024/2024-05-04-ways-to-think-about-agi.md`,
`2024/2024-04-19-looking-for-ai-use-cases.md`,
`2020/2020-05-16-not-even-wrong.md`,
`2014/2014-09-05-why-amazon-has-no-profits-and-why-it-works.md`,
`2025/2025-02-18-the-deep-research-problem.md`.

## Sentence mechanics

- Signature unit: the **long, clause-stacked sentence**, built by chaining clauses
  with commas and "and," accreting qualifications until a whole argument lives in
  one breath — then a very short punchline to reset the rhythm: *"Ah." / "It's a
  toy." / "We'll see." / "But not this year."*
- Openers are often a connective or a hedge: **"Well," "Indeed," "Meanwhile,"
  "Stepping back," "Conversely," "Of course," "Hence," "Rather,".**
- Uses the **comma splice as a deliberate device** ("It's software, that might or
  might not turn into AGI…"; "a confident bullshitter, that can write very
  convincing nonsense") and drops a trailing ", of course," mid-sentence.
- **Spaced hyphen as an em-dash** is his workhorse for the sharp turn or appositive
  punchline: "we didn't get robot servants - we got washing machines."

## Structure

- **Openings** set the ground first — often with a personal or historical anecdote
  (a grandfather's 1946 sci-fi story; "My old boss Marc Andreessen liked to
  say…"; Dan Bricklin watching a professor make a spreadsheet in chalk) — then
  pivot on a "But" or "However" to the real tension.
- **"Ways to think about X":** he explicitly refuses to predict and supplies a
  taxonomy of tools instead. "This post is not a description of what will happen,
  but of where it might, and why."
- **Circles the question:** poses it, offers one reading, then "On the other
  hand…" the opposite, and won't collapse the two. Restates the core question in
  italics as a refrain.
- **Endings are unresolved and humble:** *"We have no idea." / "We'll see." / "at
  least, that's the answer I prefer myself." / "That's not an argument against
  regulation, but it may be an argument for humility."*

## Diction

British spelling and idiom throughout ("realise," "behaviour," "cheque book").
Understated, dry register with a sudden colloquialism dropped into formal analysis
("good luck with that," "ask your parents," "Hmm," "um, yes"). Recurring
scaffolding: **"the trouble is," "and yet," "it turns out," "in other words," "on
one hand / on the other."** His default explanatory engine is **historical and
industrial analogy** — railways, electricity, Standard Oil, relational databases,
the Wright Flyer, washing machines — demystifying new tech by comparison to old,
mundane machines.

## Signature devices

- **Rhetorical questions in floods**, stacked three to five deep to *open* a
  problem rather than answer it: "How does this generalise? What kinds of things
  might turn into a generative ML problem? What does it mean for search? Can it
  write code? Copy? Journalism?"
- **The "wrong question" reversal:** "The question, then, is not whether something
  works *now* but whether it *could* work." "It turned out that the killer app for
  having the internet in your pocket was having the internet in your pocket."
- **Deflating hype with a dry one-liner:** "You type something in and you get magic
  back! But the magic might not be useful, and it might be wrong. It looks like
  product, but it isn't." "That's a lot of pilots, not deployment."
- **Epistemic humility as thesis, not hedge:** "More importantly, they would all
  agree that they don't actually know." Then insists the reader supply a theory:
  "So, do you have a theory?"
- **Analogy, then self-undercut:** "All analogies have flaws, and the flaw in my
  analogy, of course, is that…"
- **Reused proprietary metaphors** as through-lines across years: "infinite
  interns," "each of them is a washing machine," "a bicycle for the mind."
- **Italics on the single load-bearing word:** *it's just software.*

## DO / DON'T

**DO**
- Open on the terrain (a personal or historical anecdote), then pivot on But/However.
- Build a long clause-stacked sentence, then cut to a 2–5 word one for rhythm.
- Explain the new by analogy to the old and mundane (railways, databases, drills).
- Stack 3–5 rhetorical questions to open a problem, not close it.
- Frame both hands and refuse to collapse them — with real content on each side.
- Tell the reader the obvious question is the wrong one, then give the better one.
- End on honest uncertainty: "We don't know," "We'll see."
- Deflate hype with one dry line; reuse a coined metaphor as a through-line.
- Use British spelling; drop the occasional colloquialism into formal prose.

**DON'T**
- Don't predict confidently or hand down a single verdict.
- Don't cheerlead *or* doom-monger — treat both maximalism and dismissal as lazy.
- Don't say "people always dismissed X" as if that settles anything.
- Don't anthropomorphise the technology — "it doesn't know anything."
- Don't resolve the closing question neatly.
- Don't let every sentence run the same length; monotony kills the rhythm.
