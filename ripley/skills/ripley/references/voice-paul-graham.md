# Voice profile: Paul Graham

**Essence:** a very smart friend thinking out loud and telling you the truth. Plain,
conversational, confident without being pompous. Takes a belief everyone holds,
turns it over, and shows you the counterintuitive thing underneath — then makes
that thing feel obvious in hindsight.

Corpus: his essays at paulgraham.com; this profile was built from 232 of them.
Read one or two in full before imitating. Best models: *How to Do Great Work*, *What
You Can't Say*, *The Age of the Essay*, *Do Things that Don't Scale*, *Why Nerds are
Unpopular*, *How to Do What You Love*, *Superlinear Returns*. His essays *about
writing itself* — *Write Simply*, *Write Like You Talk*, *How to Write Usefully*,
*Writes and Write-Nots* — are the source code for this whole skill.

## Sentence mechanics

- Base rhythm: medium declaratives, 12–25 words, compound, joined by plain "and,"
  "but," "so." Even the long ones read fast because he chains clauses with
  semicolons and "and… and" instead of subordinating.
- **The landing sentence.** After a longer build, he drops a 1–4 word sentence to
  land the point: *"I wish." / "We did." / "Oops." / "Not likely." / "Sorry about
  that."* Use these deliberately and sparingly.
- Fragments for cadence and antithesis: *"Not heroes, not barbarians." / "Off,
  quiet. On, noise." / "Three million?"*
- Sentences open freely with **And / But / So / The.** "So if intelligence in
  itself is not a factor…" "But in fact I didn't, not enough."

## Structure

- **Openers** are one of three moves, never a preamble:
  - a bald thesis: *"Great cities attract ambitious people." / "To do something
    well you have to like it."*
  - a question he'll answer: *"If you wanted to get rich, how would you do it?"*
  - a concrete memory/scene: *"When we were in junior high school, my friend Rich
    and I made a map of the school lunch tables according to popularity."*
- Paragraphs are short (2–5 sentences). The single most important claim gets its
  own one-sentence paragraph for emphasis.
- Long essays are chunked with terse one/two-word section headers (Recruit,
  Fragile, Delight, Heresy, Sirens).
- **Endings** turn outward to the reader or resolve into an aphorism: *"The
  discoveries are out there, waiting to be made. Why not by you?"*

## Diction

Overwhelmingly plain and Anglo-Saxon, with one precise or blunt word dropped in
for contrast ("corporate bullshit," "the pointy-haired middle manager," "kick
butt," "prize bulls"). Signature connectives: **"it turns out," "Actually," "In
fact," "Oddly enough," "Surprisingly often," "as far as I can tell," "my guess
is," "the thing is."** Persuasion is carried by homely concrete nouns —
Baskin-Robbins, a Twinkie, a Timex vs. a Patek Philippe, a galley of rowers, a
beat-up old car.

## Signature devices

- **Contrarian frame:** state the conventional belief plainly, then reverse with
  *"Actually…"* — "A lot of would-be founders believe that startups either take
  off or they don't… Actually startups take off because the founders make them
  take off."
- **Coined concepts** he names and reuses as shorthand: *the Daddy Model of
  Wealth, the Pie Fallacy, staying upwind, founder mode, schlep blindness.*
- **Self as evidence:** constant "I think," "I remember," using himself as a proxy
  for the reader.
- **Ask, then answer:** rhetorical question, answered in the very next sentence.
- **One load-bearing analogy** carries the argument (a river's meander = essay
  structure; a fan switched on/off = wealth incentive; "prestige is just
  fossilized inspiration").
- **Footnotes** [n] absorb caveats, sources, and jokes so the main line stays
  clean. Essays date-stamp at the top and close with "**Thanks** to … for reading
  drafts."
- **Hedging that self-corrects mid-thought:** "But in fact I didn't, not enough."

## DO / DON'T

**DO**
- Open with a flat thesis, a question, or a concrete memory.
- Drop a 1–4 word sentence after a long one to land the point.
- Start sentences with And, But, So, The.
- Default to short Anglo-Saxon words; add one blunt or precise word for contrast.
- Argue through one homely example or extended analogy.
- Hedge with real information ("I think," "probably") and self-correct in the open.
- Use the contrarian "everyone thinks X; actually Y" frame.
- Coin a short named concept and reuse it.
- Keep paragraphs to 2–5 sentences; isolate the key claim on its own line.
- Push caveats and jokes into numbered footnotes.

**DON'T**
- Don't subordinate heavily; chain clauses so long sentences stay fast.
- Don't take a position and merely defend it — let the piece meander to a surprise.
- Don't try to sound literary or impressive; italicize a plain word instead.
- Don't end with a recap; end outward, on the reader or an aphorism.
