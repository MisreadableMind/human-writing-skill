# Voice profile: House blend (default)

**Essence:** the default "thoughtful human" voice — what you write when the user
just wants prose that reads like a smart person wrote it, with no single
essayist's stamp on it. It is **Graham's clarity + Karlsson's concreteness +
Evans's honesty, leaning plain.** Not an average of the three (that gives mush),
and not the three taking turns (that gives whiplash). Each writer owns a
different *layer* of the same sentence, so they stack instead of fight.

Use this when no specific register is called for, or when the user says "make it
sound human / less like AI / less generic" without naming a writer. When they
*do* name one, use that profile instead.

## The layer model — the one idea that makes blending work

A blended voice fails in two ways. Averaged, it goes bland — every edge sanded
off until it sounds like careful nobody. Alternated, it lurches — a lyrical scene,
then a dry analyst clause, then a punchy aphorism, and the reader gets seasick.

The fix: the three writers don't operate on the same axis, so don't make them
compete. Give each one a layer:

| Layer | Owner | What it controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Skeleton** | Graham | The opener, the close, paragraph length, sentence economy, the contrarian turn. The *shape*. |
| **Texture** | Karlsson | The one concrete scene or example, sensory detail, real named things, the load-bearing image. The *ground*. |
| **Stance** | Evans | How sure you're allowed to sound. Honest uncertainty, both-hands where the truth is genuinely two-sided, no fake verdict. The *epistemics*. |

A single sentence can carry all three at once: a plain Graham frame, a Karlsson
image inside it, an Evans caveat on the end. They live at different heights of the
sentence, so stacking them reads as one voice, not three.

**Rhythm is the shared floor.** All three writers independently insist on the same
thing — vary sentence length hard, chain clauses with "and… and" and semicolons,
then drop a short line to land it. So rhythm isn't a layer anyone owns; it's the
ground all three stand on. Get it right and the blend coheres.

## The transform — six passes

Run these in order on any draft (yours or the user's). Each pass does one job;
don't try to do them at once.

**Pass 1 — Strip.** Before adding any voice, remove what's actively fighting it.
Delete every AI tell in `anti-ai-tells.md`: throat-clearing openers, not-just-X-but-Y,
empty inflation (crucial, robust, seamless, unlock, leverage), delve-class words,
signpost stacking (Firstly/Moreover/Furthermore), blanket hedging, recap
conclusions, weasel attribution. Rule: if you can delete a sentence and lose no
information, it was scaffolding — delete it. This pass is voice-neutral cleanup and
it comes first, because you can't hear the voice under the noise.

**Pass 2 — Set the skeleton (Graham).** Fix the two ends and the spacing.
- **Opener:** replace any preamble with one of three moves — a flat thesis
  (*"Most companies buying AI can't tell you what it's for."*), a real question you
  then answer, or a concrete scene. Never "In today's world," never a summary of
  what's coming.
- **Close:** turn outward to the reader, or land on one earned line. Cut any "In
  summary / Ultimately / At the end of the day" recap. The reader was there.
- **Spacing:** break into short paragraphs, 2–5 sentences. Put the single most
  important claim on its own one-sentence line.

**Pass 3 — Ground it (Karlsson).** Hunt every abstraction and give it *one*
concrete thing. Not a stack of examples — one homely, specific, often physical
image, and if it's good, let it recur as the through-line. Replace "companies
struggle to adopt new tools" with a person who bought a beautiful set of chisels
before deciding to make anything. Name real people, real texts, real objects where
you can; specificity is what makes it believable. One vivid example beats three
general claims.

**Pass 4 — Make it honest (Evans).** Two moves, in this order.
- First, *strengthen*. Push each claim to the strongest version that's still true.
  Vague isn't safe, it's empty.
- Then, *qualify — but only for real*. Where the truth is genuinely uncertain or
  two-sided, say so and mean it: frame both hands with actual content on each, or
  admit "nobody has fully figured this out yet." Where the obvious question is the
  wrong one, say which question is better. Kill fake confidence and fake hedging
  alike — the difference between a strong claim and a hedged one should carry
  information.

**Pass 5 — Tune the rhythm (shared).** Read it aloud. Vary sentence length hard:
build one long clause-stacked sentence chained with "and" and semicolons, then drop
a 1–4 word line right after to land it (*"We'll see." "It's early." "Not yet."*).
Fix everything that clanks when spoken. This pass is where a competent blend
becomes a good one.

**Pass 6 — De-muddle.** Check you didn't build a Frankenstein. One layer-owner may
*lead* a given passage, but the register shouldn't flip sentence to sentence. Then
strip each writer's extreme tics that don't belong in a neutral blend (see the
table below). When two impulses genuinely conflict, **lean plain** — Graham wins
ties.

## What to keep, what to drop

Pull the load-bearing move from each writer; leave the personal mannerism behind.

| From | Keep | Drop in a neutral blend |
|---|---|---|
| **Graham** | Flat-thesis openers, landing sentences, short paragraphs, contrarian "everyone thinks X; actually Y", plain Anglo-Saxon words, starting sentences with And/But/So. | Heavy numbered footnotes, the date-stamp, "Thanks to X for reading drafts," coining named jargon you then have to maintain. |
| **Karlsson** | One concrete scene, sensory detail, real named things, a recurring load-bearing image, warm direct address, honesty about feeling *when it's load-bearing*. | Signing off "Warmly, Henrik," lyricism for its own sake, confession bolted on that doesn't earn its place, section numbering as ritual. |
| **Evans** | Honest uncertainty as the actual conclusion, both-hands on genuinely two-sided questions, the wrong-question reversal, one dry deflating line. | British spelling (match the user's convention instead), the spaced-hyphen em-dash mannerism, floods of 3–5 stacked rhetorical questions, refusing to conclude when the answer *is* actually clear. |

## Worked example

**Before (generic AI):**

> In today's rapidly evolving technological landscape, artificial intelligence has
> become an increasingly crucial tool for businesses seeking to remain competitive.
> It's important to note that while AI offers numerous benefits, there are also many
> factors to consider. Companies must carefully navigate the complexities of
> implementation to unlock the full potential of these powerful technologies.
> Ultimately, the key to success lies in striking the right balance.

**After (house blend):**

> Most companies buying AI right now can't tell you what it's for. They've bought the
> tool and they're still hunting for the job — like someone who orders a beautiful set
> of chisels before deciding to make anything. That's not a failing; it's just early.
> You type a prompt and get something polished back, and sometimes it's exactly the
> memo you needed and sometimes it's a fluent, confident, wholly invented one. Telling
> those two apart is the actual work. Nobody has fully figured out how yet.
> We'll see.

What each layer did: Graham set the flat-thesis opener and the short landing lines
(*"That's not a failing; it's just early." "We'll see."*). Karlsson grounded the
abstraction in one physical image (the chisels) instead of "navigate the
complexities." Evans supplied the stance — the strong claim up front, then the
honest "nobody has figured it out yet" as the real conclusion, not a hedge. Rhythm
carries it: a long chained sentence, then three short ones.

## DO / DON'T

**DO**
- Run the six passes in order; strip first, tune rhythm last.
- Let each writer own a layer (shape / ground / stance), stacked in the same sentence.
- Keep one concrete image running as the through-line.
- State the strong claim, *then* qualify only where the uncertainty is real.
- Vary sentence length hard; land long builds with a short line.
- When two impulses fight, lean plain — Graham breaks the tie.

**DON'T**
- Don't average the three into edgeless mush, or alternate registers sentence to sentence.
- Don't import a writer's personal mannerism (Henrik's sign-off, Evans's British
  spelling, PG's footnote apparatus) just because you're borrowing his move.
- Don't manufacture both-hands uncertainty where the answer is actually clear —
  that's Evans-cosplay, not honesty.
- Don't decorate; every image has to carry an idea. If it's only pretty, cut it.
