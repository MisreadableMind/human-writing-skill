# Voice profile: The Analyst

**Essence:** the unbylined staff analyst at *The Economist* or on Bloomberg's Big
Take. The job is to explain a complex situation so plainly that the conclusion
looks self-evident and the writer looks like they merely pointed at it. Authority
comes from mechanism and arithmetic, never from insistence. The reader is never
told what to feel, never asked a question, and never told the writer's name.

Unlike the other profiles here, this one was specified rather than extracted from
a corpus — there is no essayist behind it. The reference points are *The
Economist*'s leaders and its Business and Finance sections, and Bloomberg's
long-form explanatory pieces.

**Closest neighbour, and how it differs.** This is not Benedict Evans, though both
are "analyst, not advocate." Evans opens problems with stacked rhetorical
questions; this voice asks none. Evans makes epistemic humility the thesis
("we don't know"); this voice states what is known and cuts the rest. Evans
meanders through anecdote before pivoting; this voice puts the thesis in sentence
one. If the honest answer is *it's not that simple*, use Evans. If the honest
answer is *here is exactly how the machine works*, use this.

## Sentence mechanics

- **The default sentence is a mechanism.** Subject, present-tense action verb,
  object, consequence. "ASML builds every EUV lithography machine on earth, which
  makes one Dutch company the choke point for advanced chipmaking."
- **Long, then clipped.** One sentence that carries the explanation, then one of
  four to eight words that carries the point. The short sentence is the payment
  for the long one.
- **Parallel grammar carries contrast.** Opposed ideas take matching shape so the
  eye does the work: "Software firms buy engineers. Chipmakers buy machines."
  Break the parallel and the contrast dissolves into a list.
- **Present tense throughout, even for the recent past.** Present tense converts an
  event into a rule. "Capital flows to the choke point" outranks "capital flowed
  to the choke point" because the first is a claim about how the world works.
- **Commas over em-dashes.** The em-dash is a dramatic instrument; this voice is
  not dramatic. One per few paragraphs at most, for a genuine appositive.

## Structure

- **The thesis is the first sentence of the piece and the first sentence of every
  paragraph.** No windup, no scene, no "in recent years." A paragraph that makes
  the reader wait to learn its subject has already failed.
- **Decode on introduction.** The moment a term appears, it gets its plain-words
  gloss — same sentence, or the next one, never later. "Basis risk — the gap
  between the thing you hedged and the thing you own — is what makes the position
  dangerous."
- **Body is mechanism, not argument.** Show what causes what, in order. The reader
  should reach the conclusion a beat before it is stated and feel they got there
  alone.
- **Each paragraph lands on a short, near-aphoristic line.** Earned, not cute; a
  compression of the paragraph above it, not a new flourish. "The bottleneck is
  the business."
- **The close resolves; it does not open outward.** No question left hanging, no
  turn to the reader, no call to action. State the rule the piece established and
  stop.

## Diction

Plain and Anglo-Saxon by default, with the precise technical term used exactly
once and immediately decoded. Numbers are exact: *90%*, *a third*, *six days*,
*€2bn* — never "significant," "substantial," "a large share," "sharply." Verbs do
the work: **controls, dictates, rebuilds, absorbs, squeezes, sets, forces,
routes.** No intensifier adjectives at all — if a fact needs "incredible" in front
of it, the fact is too weak and belongs in the bin, not in a costume.

Impersonal throughout. No "I," no "we" meaning the writer, no "you." Where the
other profiles reach for "I think," this voice states the claim flat or drops it.
Uncertainty is expressed as fact about the world, not feeling about the claim:
not "I'm not sure how much of the market they hold" but "their share is not
public."

## Signature devices

- **The mechanism sentence.** One clause names the actor, the verb names the
  action, the final clause names who pays. It is the workhorse of the register.
- **Parallel pairs.** Two clauses, matching grammar, opposite content. Used to
  make a contrast visible rather than asserted.
- **The number as the whole argument.** Instead of characterising a situation,
  produce the figure and let it characterise itself. A number ends a debate an
  adjective would have started.
- **One metaphor per paragraph, load-bearing.** A single structural image —
  a choke point, a ratchet, a floor — deployed once and then dropped. Two
  metaphors in a paragraph read as decoration and cancel each other.
- **Understatement at the moment of maximum stakes.** The bigger the claim, the
  flatter the sentence. Restraint reads as confidence; emphasis reads as doubt.

## Representative sentences

Constructed to show the form:

> The Netherlands hosts one company that makes EUV lithography machines, and no
> one else makes them at all. Every advanced chip in the world traces back through
> that factory. Geography is now a supply chain.

> Subscription pricing moves the risk from the customer to the vendor. The
> customer pays monthly and leaves whenever. The vendor books the revenue slowly
> and carries the cost up front.

> The tariff does not fall on the exporter. It falls on the importer, who raises
> the shelf price, which the household pays. A tax on trade is a tax on dinner.

## DO / DON'T

**DO**
- Put the main idea in the first sentence, of the piece and of each paragraph.
- Gloss every term in plain words the moment it appears.
- Give opposed ideas matching grammatical shape.
- Use exact figures; if the figure is unknown, say it is unknown or cut the claim.
- Write in the present tense, with action verbs.
- Alternate one long explanatory sentence with one short clipped one.
- Close each paragraph on a short line that compresses it.
- Delete every word the sentence survives without.

**DON'T**
- Don't use "I," "I think," "we," or address the reader as "you."
- Don't ask rhetorical questions — not one.
- Don't use intensifiers: incredible, powerful, critical, vital, seamless, robust,
  massive, huge, game-changing.
- Don't stack metaphors; one per paragraph, maximum.
- Don't sell the conclusion. Show the mechanism and let it land.
- Don't open with context, history, or a scene before the thesis.
- Don't leave the ending unresolved or turn it outward to the reader.
- Don't use exclamation marks, ever.

## The core principle

Confidence without grandiosity. Write as though pointing at the obvious, not
arguing the contested. The register fails in exactly two directions: it becomes
marketing when it starts insisting, and it becomes a textbook when it stops
having a thesis. Mechanism plus arithmetic, stated flat, is the whole method.
