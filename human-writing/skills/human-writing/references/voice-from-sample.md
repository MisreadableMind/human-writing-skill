# Voice: match the user's own writing

The strongest voice target isn't Graham, Evans, Karlsson, or even the house blend —
it's the user's own. When someone says "make it sound like me," "more like me," or
hands you a sample of their past writing, that sample **overrides every default in
this skill.** Match a real person over an archetype whenever you can.

This isn't a fifth fixed profile. It's a procedure for building a profile on the
spot from whatever they give you.

## Getting a sample

If the user wants their own voice but hasn't given you one, ask. A few hundred words
of their real, unedited writing is enough — an old blog post, a long email, a Slack
thread, a doc they wrote. Best if it's the same register as the target: don't
calibrate a landing page off their text messages.

They can hand it over inline ("here's how I write: …") or as a file path. If nothing
is available and they still want "me," say plainly that you can't match a voice you
haven't seen, and fall back to the house blend rather than guessing.

## Read the sample first — before writing a word

Read it once for feel, then again for mechanics. Note, concretely:

- **Sentence length and rhythm.** Short and clipped? Long and winding? Mixed — and
  where do the short ones land?
- **Word level.** Plain and Anglo-Saxon, or reaching for the formal word? Do they
  swear, joke, use slang? Do they say "stuff" and "things," or "elements" and
  "components"?
- **How paragraphs open.** Straight into the point, or a beat of context first?
- **Punctuation habits.** Dashes? Parentheticals? Semicolons? Ellipses? One-line
  paragraphs?
- **Transitions.** Explicit connectors ("that said," "anyway," "so"), or do they
  just start the next thought?
- **Verbal tics and pet phrases.** Recurring openers, favorite words, a way of
  hedging or emphasizing that's unmistakably theirs.
- **Stance.** Opinionated or measured? Do they show uncertainty or commit? First
  person, or held at a distance?

Write these down for yourself before drafting. You're building a one-off voice
profile the same shape as the ones in `references/` — just sourced from them instead
of from an essayist.

## Match it — don't just remove the AI tells

The point is positive imitation, not neutral cleanup. Stripping the tells gets you a
blank, correct voice. Matching the sample gets you *theirs*.

- If they write short sentences, don't hand back long ones — not even elegant ones.
- If they say "stuff," don't upgrade it to "elements."
- If they never use semicolons, don't introduce them.
- If they open cold, don't add a warm-up line.
- **Keep their tics.** The pet phrase you'd instinctively "correct" is often the
  single most *them* thing in the piece.

The ten principles still run underneath — you're still cutting empty hedging, still
preferring the concrete, still reading it aloud. But where a principle and the
sample disagree on a *surface* choice — their beloved em-dash, their comma splices,
their favorite slightly-fancy word — the sample wins. It's their voice, not yours.

## Sanity check

Read your draft and the sample back to back. **Could they have written your
version?** If a line would make them stop and think "I wouldn't say that," rewrite
it as what they *would* say. That one test — theirs, not merely non-AI — is the
whole job.
