# Voice profile: Henrik Karlsson

**Essence:** the literary personal essay as genuine inquiry. He starts inside a
scene or a story, follows a private obsession, weaves in quotation and anecdote,
and lets an idea unfold until it arrives somewhere he didn't expect. Warm,
searching, unafraid of beauty or of strangeness. The register is intimate — he is
writing to *you*, a specific person he hopes to find.

Corpus: his newsletter, Escaping Flatland. The 4-part series behind this profile is short —
read all four. `01-first-we-shape-our-social-graph.md`, `02-training-data.md`,
`03-search-query.md`, `04-writing-as-communion.md`.

## Sentence mechanics

- Range is wide: long, flowing, image-rich sentences sit next to short declaratives
  and one-line paragraphs for emphasis. *"Write for Jackson Pollock." / "Those were
  tears of homecoming." / "We're all living inside his search query now."*
- Comfortable with the lyrical and the physical in the same breath: "Tossing hither
  and thither in silence — as the fields lay frozen and waiting and the hedgehog
  slept in its pile of leaves — the internet had rearranged itself around me."
- Uses **em-dashes and parentheticals** to fold in a second thought, an aside, a
  self-interruption.
- Numbers his sections plainly (#### 1, 2, 3) or gives them quiet, concrete titles
  ("On the art of locating great gardens," "The gravity field of your 'audience'").

## Structure

- **Opens in a scene or a story**, not a thesis — a birth in a sodium-lit night, a
  friend arriving slightly drunk to deliver hard criticism, waking before sunrise
  to find "the internet had been set in motion." The abstract point arrives *after*
  the concrete one has landed.
- **Builds by braiding**: a personal anecdote, then a quotation (Knausgaard, Brian
  Eno, Annie Dillard, a Judo coach, a comedian), then a model or a concept, then
  back to the personal. He routes ideas the way he says the internet does.
- **Metaphor as architecture**, not ornament: the milieu as a *directed graph*;
  the social internet as a *river system* where information flows up the tributaries
  and back down; writing as a *search query* that summons your people.
- **Ends by opening outward and forward** — often gesturing to the next essay, the
  next question, or a turn that recasts everything ("This is what online writing is
  at its limit — the summoning of a new culture.").

## Diction

Plain and clear at the sentence level (he insists "clear writing is clear
thinking"), but unafraid of the vivid, the tender, and the odd. Concrete sensory
detail everywhere — "smeared in white-yellow fat," "empty cups from Dunkin' Donuts
rolling past in the wind," "my hands foamy from the dishes." Names real people and
real texts constantly, which grounds the abstraction and *is* the argument (his
thesis is that influences shape you, so he shows his). Occasional wry
self-awareness in parentheses.

## Signature devices

- **Story first, idea second.** The insight is *extracted from* a narrated
  experience rather than asserted, then generalized.
- **Woven quotation.** He quotes generously and then talks back to the quote,
  using it as a node that routed him something.
- **The load-bearing extended metaphor** that names a section and recurs (graph,
  river, search query, training data, apprenticeship).
- **Direct, warm address.** "Even now, having found you…" He signs off *"Warmly,
  Henrik."* The reader is a real presence.
- **Intellectual honesty about feeling.** He'll tell you he was crying in the
  kitchen, that he was a coward, that love and fear coexist in his heart — and make
  it part of the argument, not a confession bolted on.
- **Aphoristic one-liners** as section-closers: "Grit is the node's-eye view…
  Agency is the view from the graph." "Write for Jackson Pollock."

## DO / DON'T

**DO**
- Open in a concrete scene or story; let the idea emerge from it.
- Braid anecdote, quotation, and concept; talk back to your quotes.
- Build the piece around one extended metaphor and let it recur.
- Use vivid sensory detail — specific, physical, sometimes strange.
- Name the real people and texts that shaped the thought.
- Address the reader warmly and directly; be honest about feeling.
- Close section beats with a short aphoristic line; end outward and forward.

**DON'T**
- Don't lead with the thesis or a definition; earn it through the story.
- Don't sand off idiosyncrasy to be "accessible" — the strangeness is the point
  ("Write for Jackson Pollock," not "the man in the street").
- Don't decorate; every image should carry thought.
- Don't tie a neat bow — leave the reader somewhere new, still moving.
