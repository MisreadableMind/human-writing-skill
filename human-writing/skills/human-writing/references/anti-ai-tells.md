# Anti-AI tells: the patterns that mark text as machine-written

Sounding human is mostly *subtraction*. A model's default prose is grammatical and
organized but hollow — it hedges everything, decorates instead of arguing, and
signposts a structure it never fills. Strip these patterns out and you're already
most of the way there.

Rule of thumb: **if you can delete a sentence and lose no information, it was a
tell. Delete it.**

---

## 1. Throat-clearing openers

The piece announces itself instead of starting.

- ✗ "In today's fast-paced world, communication is more important than ever."
- ✗ "In an era defined by rapid technological change…"
- ✗ "In the ever-evolving landscape of software development…"
- ✗ "Let's dive in." / "Buckle up." / "Let's explore."
- ✓ Start on the actual thing: a claim, a question, a scene, a number.

Every one of these can be cut whole. The reader knows what world they live in.

## 2. The "not just X, but Y" tic

The single most recognizable AI cadence.

- ✗ "It's not just a tool; it's a way of thinking."
- ✗ "This isn't about writing code — it's about solving problems."
- ✗ "More than a feature, it's a philosophy."
- ✓ Say the one true thing directly. If X and Y both matter, make each a real,
  separate sentence with content in it.

## 3. Empty inflation words

Adjectives and verbs that sound important and mean nothing. Cut or replace with
something concrete.

> crucial, vital, pivotal, essential, key, powerful, robust, seamless,
> comprehensive, revolutionary, groundbreaking, game-changing, cutting-edge,
> state-of-the-art, world-class, unlock, elevate, empower, harness, leverage,
> supercharge, streamline, revolutionize, transform, navigate the complexities of,
> at its core, when it comes to, in the realm of, the world of, a testament to,
> plays a crucial/vital role, stands as, serves as.

- ✗ "This powerful tool unlocks seamless collaboration."
- ✓ "Two people can now edit the same file at once."

## 4. Delve-class vocabulary

Words that are technically fine but statistically scream *model*.

> delve, tapestry, testament, underscore, boasts, realm, plethora, myriad,
> foster, showcase, intricate, multifaceted, nuanced (as filler), embark,
> beacon, landscape (figurative), symphony, treasure trove.

Prefer the plain word. Not "delve into" — "look at," "dig into," "get into."

## 5. Signpost stacking

Connective words used as filler, not real sequence.

- ✗ "Firstly… Secondly… Moreover… Furthermore… Additionally… In addition…"
- ✓ Use a signpost only when there's a genuine list the reader needs numbered.
  Otherwise let "And," "But," "So" carry the flow. Real writers rarely say
  "Furthermore."

## 6. Hedge-everything

The vague trap: it feels safe and says nothing. (Different from *skilled*
qualification, which carries real information — see below.)

- ✗ "It's important to note that there are many factors to consider."
- ✗ "It's worth mentioning that results may vary."
- ✗ "Ultimately, whether X is right depends on a variety of factors."
- ✓ Make the strongest claim that's true, then qualify it precisely: "This works
  for teams under ten; past that I'm not sure."

**Skilled qualification vs. empty hedging:** "I think," "probably," "roughly,"
"for teams under ten" all narrow the claim and add information — keep them. "It's
important to note," "it should be mentioned," "in many cases," "to some extent"
add nothing — cut them.

## 7. Mechanical rhythm and punctuation tics

- **Rule-of-three everything:** "innovative, scalable, and robust." One vivid word
  usually beats three vague ones.
- **The reflexive dramatic em-dash** on every third sentence — the "— and that's
  the real point —" cadence. (Dashes are fine; the *reflex* is the tell.)
- **Every sentence the same medium length.** Vary hard: chain a long one, then cut
  to three words.
- **Perfectly parallel bullet lists** where prose would carry the argument better.

## 8. Recap conclusions and moralizing coda

- ✗ "In summary, we have seen that…" / "In conclusion…" / "Ultimately…"
- ✗ "At the end of the day, remember that the real treasure was the journey."
- ✓ The reader was just there — don't re-narrate. End by turning outward, landing
  one earned line, or leaving the question honestly open.

## 9. Weasel attribution

- ✗ "Studies show…" / "Experts agree…" / "It is widely believed…" / "Research
  suggests…" — with nothing named.
- ✓ Name the study, the person, the number — or drop the appeal to authority and
  make the argument yourself. Vague authority is worse than none.

## 10. Fake balance and false symmetry

Both-sidesing that adds no content, so the piece can avoid committing.

- ✗ "There are pros and cons. On one hand it's useful; on the other, it has
  drawbacks."
- ✓ If you present two sides, put real, specific content on each (this is what
  Benedict Evans does well — genuine tension, not a shrug). Otherwise, take the
  side the evidence supports.

## 11. Over-explaining the obvious / padding

Models restate the prompt, define common words, and add "as we can see" filler.

- ✗ "Collaboration, which means working together with others…"
- ✗ "As we can see from the above, it is clear that…"
- ✓ Trust the reader. Cut the definition of any word they already know.

## 12. Emoji-bulleted listicle as a substitute for thought

A wall of ✅/🚀/💡 bullets often hides the fact that no argument was made. Bullets
are for genuinely parallel items. If the ideas connect, write sentences.

## 13. Copula avoidance

Dodging plain "is / are / has" for something that sounds busier.

- ✗ "Gallery 825 serves as the exhibition space and boasts 3,000 square feet."
- ✓ "Gallery 825 is the exhibition space. It has 3,000 square feet."

"Is" isn't a weak verb, it's an honest one. Watch for *serves as, stands as,
represents, boasts, features, offers* standing in for it.

## 14. Superficial "-ing" tails

Present-participle phrases bolted to the end of a sentence to fake depth.

- ✗ "The palette uses blue and gold, symbolizing the coast and reflecting the
  community's connection to the land."
- ✓ "The palette uses blue and gold. The architect chose them to echo the coast."

If the "-ing" clause makes a real claim, give it its own sentence. If it doesn't,
cut it. Usual suspects: *highlighting, underscoring, reflecting, symbolizing,
showcasing, ensuring, fostering.*

## 15. Elegant variation (synonym cycling)

The same thing renamed every sentence to dodge repetition — an artifact of the
model's repetition penalty.

- ✗ "The protagonist… The main character… The central figure… The hero…"
- ✓ Name it once and then use a pronoun. Repeating the right word beats a parade
  of synonyms; the reader stops trusting that these are all the same person.

## 16. False ranges

"From X to Y" where X and Y aren't the ends of any real scale.

- ✗ "…from the Big Bang to dark matter, from the birth of stars to the dance of
  galaxies."
- ✓ "It covers the Big Bang, star formation, and dark matter."

A range promises a spectrum. If there isn't one, it's just a list in costume.

## 17. Tailing negations

A clipped "no X" fragment stuck on the end instead of written as a real clause.

- ✗ "The options come from the selected item, no guessing."
- ✓ "The options come from the selected item, so you don't have to guess."

## 18. Formula aphorisms

Ordinary claims dressed as reusable maxims: *X is the Y of Z, the language of…,
the currency of…, the architecture of…, X becomes a trap.*

- ✗ "Symmetry is the language of trust."
- ✓ "Symmetric layouts feel more predictable, so people trust them faster."

Tension worth holding: an *earned, specific* aphorism is a signature move (Henrik's
"Write for Jackson Pollock," PG's "prestige is just fossilized inspiration"). A
*formulaic* one is a tell. Same shape, opposite value — the test is whether it adds
precision or just ceremony.

## 19. Staccato drama

A run of short fragments engineered to sound momentous.

- ✗ "Then it arrived. No preference for symmetry. No nostalgia. The old rules were
  gone."
- ✓ Let one short sentence land the point; four in a row manufacture one.

This is the flip side of the landing sentence (principle 8). The device works
*because* it's rare. Used as a drumbeat, it becomes the tell.

## 20. Fake-candid openers

A standalone "Honestly?" / "Look," / "Here's the thing" to manufacture intimacy
before an ordinary claim.

- ✗ "Is it worth it? Honestly? It depends how often you'll use it."
- ✓ "Whether it's worth it depends on how often you'll use it."

A person actually being candid usually just says the thing.

## 21. Chatbot residue

Assistant-correspondence phrases pasted straight into the deliverable — and its
sycophantic cousin.

- ✗ "Great question! Here's an overview. I hope this helps — let me know if you'd
  like me to expand!"
- ✗ "You're absolutely right, that's an excellent point."
- ✓ Just deliver the content. Cut the greeting, the offer to continue, the praise.

We're an agent handing prose to a human. This is our single most likely tell —
scan for it every time.

## 22. Speculative gap-filling and cutoff disclaimers

When the facts aren't there, models do one of two things: flag their own ignorance
("as of my last update…"), or invent plausible filler. For a person with no
sources, the guess almost always lands on the same stock phrases.

- ✗ "Details are scarce, but she likely studied locally and prefers to stay out of
  the spotlight."
- ✓ "Her early life isn't documented in the sources I have." — or cut the sentence.

Say what isn't known. Never dress a guess as a fact. This one is integrity, not
just style.

## 23. Diff-anchored writing

Docs or comments that narrate a *change* instead of describing the thing as it is.
(Fine for changelogs, release notes, migration guides — wrong everywhere else.)

- ✗ "This function replaces the old loop that caused O(n²) performance."
- ✓ "This function uses a hash map for O(1) lookups."

The reader wants to know what it does now, not what it used to do. If the doc reads
like it's narrating your last commit, rewrite it to describe the present.

## 24. Mechanical formatting tells

Format habits that read as generated — especially in docs, READMEs, and landing
copy, which this skill explicitly targets:

- **Inline-header lists:** `**Performance:** performance has been improved…` — fold
  it into prose, or into a clean list that doesn't restate itself.
- **Reflexive boldface** on every key phrase. Bold is for the one word that would
  change the reader's mind, not for decoration.
- **Title Case Headings.** Use sentence case: "Getting started," not "Getting
  Started."
- **Fragmented headers:** a heading followed by a one-line paragraph that just
  restates the heading. Cut the restatement; start on the real content.
- **Predicate hyphens:** "a high-quality report" keeps the hyphen; "the report is
  high quality" drops it. Models hyphenate both; people don't.

## 25. Excitement injection

Telling the reader how to feel about the thing instead of describing the thing. This
is the single most common tell in machine prose after throat-clearing, and it hides
inside sentences that otherwise look clean.

- ✗ "The interesting part is that it caches the result."
- ✗ "Remarkably, throughput doubled." / "Surprisingly, nobody noticed."
- ✗ "And here's the exciting bit." / "What's fascinating here is…"
- ✓ "It caches the result." / "Throughput doubled." / "Nobody noticed."

**It is not the writer's job to decide what the reader finds interesting.** State the
fact precisely enough and the reader will get there on their own — or won't, which is
their right. Every evaluative adverb you add is a small insult: it says the fact
couldn't carry itself.

Usual suspects, all deletable without loss: *remarkably, surprisingly, interestingly,
notably, importantly, strikingly, incredibly, truly, quietly* ("quietly became the
standard"), *fascinating, exciting, powerful, striking, wild, crazy, mind-blowing.*

Also the sentence-shaped versions: "The best part is…", "Here's what's interesting…",
"This is where it gets good.", "Wait until you see…"

**Where the line is.** Three things look like this tell and aren't:

- **"Actually" and "genuinely" marking a contrast**, not adding heat. Graham's
  contrarian turn — *"Everyone thinks X. Actually Y."* — is a documented signature
  move, and "genuinely two-sided" distinguishes real from apparent. Both carry
  information. *"This is genuinely useful"* doesn't. Test: is there a contrast in
  play, or just emphasis?
- **Surprise the writer actually had, stated as a fact about them.** "I expected the
  opposite" is a claim about you that was checkable. "Surprisingly, X" is an
  instruction to the reader.
- **An adjective a number earned right beside it.** "The runaway favourite" survives
  when "recommended 4 times" is sitting in front of it.

In every case the test is the same: is the evaluation doing work the fact can't?

Cut every one of these on the revision pass. If deleting the adverb leaves the
sentence unchanged in meaning, that's your answer.

## 26. The one-word question pivot

A fragment-question used as a hinge between thoughts.

- ✗ "The catch? It only works offline." / "The result? Nobody used it." / "Why does
  this matter? Because…"
- ✓ "The catch is that it only works offline." / "Nobody used it."

Principle 9 endorses asking a real question and answering it — a whole question, that
a reader might genuinely have. This is the degraded version: a noun with a question
mark stapled on, manufacturing suspense across a gap of two words.

## 27. The announced analogy

Flagging a comparison instead of making one.

- ✗ "Think of it like a library card for your data." / "It's basically the Uber of
  invoicing." / "Imagine a highway where…"
- ✓ "The token is a library card. It proves you're allowed in, and it expires."

If the analogy works, it works without an usher. "Think of it like" also tends to
introduce the weakest kind — a comparison that decorates rather than explains. The
test from principle 4 still applies: does the image carry an *idea*, or is it there
because the paragraph felt abstract?

## 28. The first person with no biography

Model prose uses "I" freely and none of it is personal. The "I" thinks, notices,
suspects, and finds things useful — but never has a Tuesday, a job, a specific thing
it got wrong, or a person it argued with.

- ✗ "I've found that teams often struggle with this." (Found where? Which teams?)
- ✗ "In my experience, the simplest approach usually wins."
- ✓ "We shipped the complicated version first and spent a month unwinding it."

Personal-sounding without being personal. The fix is either a real particular or no
first person at all — a flat claim beats a fake memory. And when you're drafting in
someone else's voice, the particulars have to be theirs; see
`concrete-without-inventing.md`.

## 29. The scheduled concession

"To be fair," "That said," "Of course, this isn't for everyone" — deployed on rhythm
rather than where the argument is actually weak. Every claim gets exactly one
counterweight and the piece stays perfectly level.

Real concession is asymmetric. Most claims get none; the one shaky claim gets a long,
uncomfortable one that may not resolve. If the draft concedes on schedule, it isn't
conceding, it's pacing. (Tell #10 is the paragraph version, `structure-tells.md` #7
the whole-piece version.)

## 30. Announced directness

Declaring a manner instead of just having it.

- ✗ "Let me be clear:" / "To be blunt:" / "No fluff — here's the truth." / "I'll say
  the quiet part out loud."
- ✓ Say the thing.

Related to the fake-candid opener (#20), but distinct: that one manufactures
intimacy, this one manufactures courage. Both are announcements standing in for the
act. A person being blunt is recognisable by the bluntness, not by the label.

## A quick before/after

**Before (model default):**
> In today's rapidly evolving digital landscape, effective communication has
> become more crucial than ever. It's not just about conveying information — it's
> about forging meaningful connections. Moreover, studies show that clear writing
> can significantly enhance engagement. Ultimately, by leveraging these powerful
> strategies, you can unlock your full potential as a communicator.

**After (human):**
> Most writing is worse than it needs to be because the writer is trying to sound
> impressive. Don't. Say what you mean in the plainest words that carry it, and
> read it aloud before you send it. If a sentence isn't something you'd say to a
> friend, rewrite it until it is. That one habit will put you ahead of most people
> who write for a living.

Same length. The second one has a claim, a reason, an instruction, and a stake —
and not one word you could delete without losing something.

## Before you cut: what is NOT a tell

Sounding human is subtraction, but over-subtraction guts good prose. None of these,
on its own, means a text is machine-written. Don't flag them in isolation:

- **Clean grammar and consistent style.** Most published writing is edited. Polish
  isn't a confession.
- **Formal or academic vocabulary.** We flag *specific* words (delve, tapestry) —
  not all long words. "Ostensibly" and "constituent" are just words; don't flatten
  them.
- **A lone em-dash, one "however," curly quotes, a single short punchy sentence.**
  All ordinary. Word and macOS auto-curl quotes by default; journalists love
  dashes; humans use clipped sentences to land a point.
- **Bland or dry prose.** Dryness without the specific tells is just dry writing.
- **Unsourced claims.** Most of the web is unsourced. It proves nothing.

The signal is a **cluster**, not a lone tell. One em-dash is nothing; an em-dash
plus rule-of-three plus "vibrant tapestry" plus a "Conclusion" section is a
confession. When a single marker is all you have, leave the text alone.

## Signs of a human — preserve these

The flip side of the list above. When you see these, lean toward leaving the prose
be; editing them out is exactly how you make human writing sound like a model:

- **Specific, hard-to-fabricate detail.** A real address, an odd quote, "the lawyer
  who worked upstairs from my dentist." Models round specifics off; people hoard
  them.
- **Mixed feelings and unresolved tension.** "I think it's mostly good, but it
  bothers me and I can't say why." Models default to clean takes.
- **Dated, era-bound references.** Slang, memes, and in-jokes that place the writer
  in a specific time and scene. Models lag by a year or more.
- **Varied sentence length.** Real writing swings short-to-long; model prose drifts
  toward one even, mid-length cadence.
- **Genuine asides and self-corrections.** "(I keep wanting to say 'almost' here,
  but it really was certain.)" Models rarely interrupt themselves like this.

If a rewrite would delete one of these, don't. That detail is usually the reason
the piece sounds like a person.
